How the numbers work

Swiss database. Nutrient values come from the Swiss Food Composition Database (naehrwertdaten.ch), Federal Food Safety and Veterinary Office (FSVO), used under its free-use terms with attribution. Diet, gluten, pork, quality and portion fields are not part of the FSVO data: ProteinMaxx assigns them by rules based on each food's category and name. Prepared and mixed foods are tagged "check recipe".

Sources. Foods marked USDA use values from USDA FoodData Central (SR Legacy), per 100 g of edible portion in the stated state. Protein powders and nutritional yeast vary by brand; those rows are typical label values and are tagged "label avg". Check your own product's label for those.

Protein per calorie barely changes with cooking. Cooking drives off water, which raises protein and calories per 100 g together. Raw chicken breast (22.5 g, 120 kcal) and roasted (31 g, 165 kcal) both land near 18.8 g per 100 kcal. Weight-based columns do change, so match the state to how you weigh your food.

% kcal from protein uses 4 kcal per gram. USDA calories use food-specific factors, so this percentage can differ by a few points from other tools and is capped at 100%.

Quality score is an approximate amino-acid score based on published DIAAS and PDCAAS values, capped at 1.0. Animal proteins and soy score high. Grains, nuts and wheat gluten are limited by lysine. Collagen scores 0 because it lacks tryptophan, so it cannot cover protein needs alone. Values for vegetables, fruit and seeds are rough estimates. Mixing plant sources (grains with legumes) covers the gaps in practice.

Density matters too. Spinach has an excellent ratio but you would need about a kilogram to get 30 g of protein. Use the "Min protein per 100 g" slider or the target column to keep results practical.

Portions for Swiss foods are typical amounts chosen by ProteinMaxx (for example 120 g for meat or fish, 30 g for cheese), not FSVO values.
